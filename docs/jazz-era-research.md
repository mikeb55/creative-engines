## Swing (1930s–1940s)

### Chord vocabulary
- Common: 6, maj6, 6/9, 7, 9, 7b9, m6, m7, dim7, 7sus4.  
- Occasional: maj7 (shows up late 1940s), m7b5 as ii in minor, 13.  
- Rare: altered dominants beyond b9, simple #11 colours, extended quartal voicings.

### Common progressions
- Functional harmony, diatonic and secondary dominants.  
- ii–V–I in major and minor, I–VI–ii–V, iii–VI–ii–V cycles.  
- 12‑bar blues (I–IV–I–I / IV–IV–I–I / V–IV–I–I or V).  
- Rhythm changes: I–VI–ii–V in A sections, circle-of-fifths bridges.  
- Turnarounds: I–vi–ii–V, I–VI7–II7–V7.  
- Limited use of tritone subs.

### Harmonic rhythm
- Typically 1–2 bars per chord in heads and dance arrangements.  
- Faster motion (2 chords per bar) in turnarounds and rhythm changes.  
- Blues may sit 4 bars on I at the top.

### Rhythmic feel and tempo range (BPM)
- Swing eighths, strong quarter‑note pulse.  
- Tempos mostly 120–220 BPM; dance music centers around medium‑up swing.  
- Ballads ~60–90, flag‑waver up‑tempo ~240+ but less common.

### Bass line style
- Acoustic bass.  
- Early swing: 2‑feel (root–5) on slower tunes; by late 1930s, 4‑to‑the‑bar walking is standard.  
- Emphasis on roots and fifths, outlining chord tones, strong downbeats.  
- Slash chords are rare; walking implies roots rather than explicit slash notation.

### Drum pattern characteristics
- Ride cymbal emerging but hi‑hat on 2 and 4 very prominent.  
- Simple swung ride pattern (“ding‑ding‑da‑ding”) with light comping on snare.  
- Kick drum reinforces quarter notes or accents figures with the band.  
- Drumming is time‑keeping oriented, relatively low interaction compared to later eras.

### Horn voicing density
- Dense: big bands (3–5 saxes, 3–4 trumpets, 2–4 trombones).  
- Block‑chord section voicings, close and drop‑2 voicings in saxes, unison + harmonised riffs.  
- Call‑and‑response between sections is common.

### Key distinguishing features vs adjacent eras
- Compared to pre‑swing: more consistent 4‑beat feel and codified big‑band arranging.  
- Compared to Bebop: simpler chord extensions, slower harmonic rhythm, more riff‑based writing, less chromatic substitution, more dance‑oriented tempos.

## Bebop (1940s–1950s)

### Chord vocabulary
- Common: maj7, 6/9, m7, m7b5, m6, 7, 9, 13, 7b9, 7#9, 7#11, 7b13, dim7, fully diminished passing chords.  
- Frequent: tritone substitutions (e.g. Db7 for G7), secondary dominants on nearly every diatonic chord.  
- Rare: very static modal vamps; harmony is busy and functional.

### Common progressions
- Dense ii–V–I usage in major and minor, often chaining: iii–VI–ii–V–I.  
- Rhythm changes, bebop blues with added ii–V cycles and chromatic approach dominants.  
- Use of backdoor ii–V (ivm7–bVII7–I).  
- Passing diminished chords between I and ii, between IV and IVm, etc.  
- Frequent chord‑scale “enclosures” via II–V cells on non‑tonic targets.

### Harmonic rhythm
- Fast: typically 1 chord per bar or 2 chords per bar; rhythm changes and bebop heads can push to 4 chords per bar.  
- Harmonic motion is continuous; very few static vamps.

### Rhythmic feel and tempo range (BPM)
- Swing eighths, often very fast.  
- Typical tempos medium‑up to up (~160–300 BPM); fast bebop around 280–340+.  
- Ballads exist but not the center of the style.  
- Phrasing is highly syncopated, with off‑beat accents and double‑time figures.

### Bass line style
- Acoustic, 4‑to‑the‑bar walking almost always.  
- Lines outline full chord changes with chromatic approach notes.  
- More linear guide‑tone and scalar motion than pure root‑5 patterns.  
- Strong emphasis on bar‑line clarity for rapid ii–V chains.

### Drum pattern characteristics
- Ride cymbal becomes primary timekeeper, hi‑hat still on 2 and 4 but lighter.  
- “Dropping bombs” on snare and bass drum — interactive accents under soloists.  
- Comping is more syncopated and conversational; less straight four‑on‑the‑floor.  
- Brushes used on ballads and some heads.

### Horn voicing density
- Medium: small combos (quartets/quintets).  
- Heads often unison or simple two‑part harmony; backgrounds sparsely voiced.  
- Emphasis on single‑line improvisation rather than thick ensemble writing.

### Key distinguishing features vs adjacent eras
- Compared to Swing: much faster tempos, denser ii–V chains, more chromatic alterations, smaller groups, more interactive rhythm section.  
- Compared to Cool Jazz: more aggressive articulation, higher tempo average, less emphasis on space and timbral subtlety.

## Cool Jazz (1950s)

### Chord vocabulary
- Common: maj7, 6/9, m7, m6, mMaj7, 7, 9, 13, m7b5, dim7, add9 colours.  
- Extensions are present but often voiced more lightly (open voicings, fewer hard b9/#9 stacks).  
- Occasional: lydian colours (maj7#11), non‑functional planing, early modal static chords.  
- Rare: relentlessly altered dominants every bar; harmonic density is generally lower than bebop/hard bop.

### Common progressions
- Still uses ii–V–I and standards repertoire, but with:  
  - smoother voice leading, more stepwise bass in arrangements,  
  - some modal/static sections,  
  - more reliance on arranged counter‑lines and less on high‑tension turnarounds.  
- Blues and rhythm changes appear but often at more moderate tempos and with less chromatic overload.

### Harmonic rhythm
- Often slower than bebop: 1–2 bars per chord is common.  
- More use of static vamps and longer pedal sections, especially in intros and interludes.  
- Arrangements may feature entire phrases over a single colour (e.g. Dm7 for 4–8 bars).

### Rhythmic feel and tempo range (BPM)
- Still swing eighths, but lighter attack; more evenness in some lines.  
- Tempos skew toward medium and medium‑slow (90–160 BPM); fewer extreme uptempos.  
- Greater use of rubato intros, ballads, and subtle dynamic contour.

### Bass line style
- Acoustic, walking in standards, but with:  
  - more use of 2‑feel and broken‑time support,  
  - more scalar and melodic bass movement,  
  - occasional pedal tones for modal sections.  
- Bass may participate in counter‑melodic ideas more than in swing.

### Drum pattern characteristics
- Light ride cymbal, less aggressive accents than bebop.  
- More use of brushes, especially on ballads and medium tunes.  
- Comping is spacious; drums often “float” rather than push.  
- Latin or straight‑eighth textures occasionally appear but are understated.

### Horn voicing density
- Sparse to medium.  
- Small groups with cool‑style voicings (e.g. non‑vibrato, soft dynamics, contrapuntal lines).  
- Arrangements favor open intervals, linear counterpoint, and careful orchestration (e.g. Mulligan, Miles’ Birth of the Cool).  
- Less dense block‑chord riffing than swing, less raw intensity than hard bop.

### Key distinguishing features vs adjacent eras
- Compared to Bebop: cooler tone, more space, slower tempos, smoother lines, more arrangement focus and timbral experimentation.  
- Compared to Hard Bop: less blues/gospel influence, less rhythmic aggression, more emphasis on subtle colour and texture than on groove intensity.

## Hard Bop (1950s–1960s)

### Chord vocabulary
- Common: maj7, 6/9, m7, m7b5, m6, 7, 9, 13, 7b9, 7#9, 7#11, 7b13, dim7, sus4, altered dominants.  
- Strong use of blues language: dominant chords with #9/b9 over I and IV, minor‑key dominants.  
- Occasional: modal sections; quartal voicings starting to appear.  
- Harmony still broadly functional, but with more color and grit than swing.

### Common progressions
- ii–V–I still central, but with:  
  - blues and minor blues forms heavily featured,  
  - “soul jazz” vamps (I7 to IV7, or minor vamps),  
  - extended turnarounds with backdoor ii–V,  
  - use of gospel‑like progressions (IV–iv–I, vi–IV–I, etc.).  
- Rhythm changes less dominant than in bebop, but present.

### Harmonic rhythm
- Mix of bebop‑like fast changes and longer funk/soul vamps.  
- Typical heads: 1 chord per bar or 2 chords per bar; solos sometimes open up into longer stretches on a single harmony.

### Rhythmic feel and tempo range (BPM)
- Strong, driving swing feel.  
- Tempos common from 110–220 BPM; blues at medium‑up are typical.  
- More emphasis on groove and backbeat than bebop; some tunes incorporate Latin or early funk inflections.

### Bass line style
- Acoustic, 4‑to‑the‑bar walking as default.  
- Lines are more groove‑inflected; walking can lean into repeated cells and chromatic enclosures.  
- On groove tunes, bass may use repetitive riffs, pedals, or ostinato patterns.

### Drum pattern characteristics
- Ride cymbal with assertive, sometimes heavier beat; backbeat emphasis stronger than in bebop.  
- More use of hard‑hitting snare accents and crash punctuation.  
- Latin grooves and shuffle feels are common; drummers drive the band aggressively.  
- Comping is active but rooted in strong groove rather than pure volatility.

### Horn voicing density
- Medium: classic quintets/sextets (trumpet + sax, sometimes trombone).  
- Heads often harmonised in thirds/sixths, or unison riffs with shout‑style hits.  
- Backgrounds behind solos can be riff‑based and fairly dense, especially on bluesy material.

### Key distinguishing features vs adjacent eras
- Compared to Cool Jazz: hotter tone, more blues/gospel influence, stronger backbeat and groove, more intense dynamics.  
- Compared to Post‑Bop: harmony more functional and blues‑based, fewer modal or free sections, less abstraction; the groove is more consistently grounded in swing or shuffle.

## Post‑Bop (1960s–1970s)

### Chord vocabulary
- Common: maj7, maj7#11, m7, m7b5, mMaj7, 7alt (7b9/#9/#11/b13), 7sus, quartal voicings, cluster voicings, slash chords.  
- Modal chords: e.g. Dm7, Fmaj7#11 held for long spans.  
- Polychords and ambiguous stackings in advanced contexts.

### Common progressions
- Blend of functional ii–V–I with modal harmony and non‑functional moves.  
- Use of pedal points, side‑slips, planing chord structures, and ambiguous key centers.  
- Tunes may alternate between modal sections and fast ii–V passages.  
- Less reliance on blues and rhythm changes; more original forms and asymmetrical structures.

### Harmonic rhythm
- Very variable:  
  - some tunes with rapid ii–V cycling,  
  - others with long modal vamps or static harmony.  
- Frequent contrasts between “busy” and “static” sections within a single piece.

### Rhythmic feel and tempo range (BPM)
- Swing remains important but with freer phrasing and metric ambiguity.  
- Tempos from ballad to very fast; also more use of odd meters (5/4, 7/4) and layered rhythmic groupings.  
- Feel can be looser, with sections that verge on free time.

### Bass line style
- Acoustic; walking, pedal, and freer line constructions all used.  
- Bass may move independently rather than simply marking roots, especially in modal contexts.  
- Broken time is common: implying pulse without strict four‑to‑the‑bar.

### Drum pattern characteristics
- Ride still central but with more metric modulation, polyrhythms, and textural playing.  
- Timekeeping and commentary blend; drums interact heavily with soloists.  
- Use of cymbal colors, rolls, and non‑traditional textures.

### Horn voicing density
- Medium to dense.  
- Small groups often use non‑traditional voicings (quartal harmony, clusters, spread wide intervals).  
- Heads may be angular, with less diatonic parallelism and more intervallic writing.

### Key distinguishing features vs adjacent eras
- Compared to Hard Bop: less strictly functional harmony, more modal/static sections, more metric variety, more openness.  
- Compared to Fusion: retains acoustic instrumentation and swing feel, with less explicit rock/funk groove and less electric timbre.

## Fusion (1970s–1980s)

### Chord vocabulary
- Common: extended tertian chords with added tensions: maj7, maj7#11, 6/9, m7, m9, m11, m7b5, 7sus, 7alt, slash chords, quartal stacks.  
- Use of static modal chords over vamps.  
- Polychords and “keyboard pad” voicings frequent.

### Common progressions
- Often modal or vamp‑based:  
  - one or two chords over long stretches,  
  - funk/fusion vamps like Em7–A7, Dm7–G7sus, etc.  
- Less emphasis on traditional standards forms; through‑composed sections and riff‑based harmony.  
- When functional, ii–V–I may be embedded inside rock/funk grooves rather than classic swing.

### Harmonic rhythm
- Often slow: many bars on a single chord or two‑chord vamp.  
- Some pieces alternate static sections with more complex harmonic movement in bridges/solos.

### Rhythmic feel and tempo range (BPM)
- Straight‑eighth feel dominates, with strong rock/funk backbeats.  
- Tempos from medium (~90) to fast (~200), but feel is driven by groove subdivisions (16th‑note funk, rock).  
- Odd meters and mixed meters are common.

### Bass line style
- Electric bass is common; acoustic still appears in some fusion variants.  
- Riff‑based lines, syncopated funk patterns, slaps, and ostinatos.  
- Less continuous walking; more pattern repetition and motivic bass figures.  
- Bass frequently outlines modal centers rather than full functional progressions.

### Drum pattern characteristics
- Rock/funk‑influenced patterns: strong backbeat on 2 and 4, straight subdivisions.  
- Use of 16th‑note hi‑hat patterns, ghost notes, and complex kick drum figures.  
- High energy, often dense; can incorporate jazz‑like fills and metric modulations over rock grooves.

### Horn voicing density
- Medium to dense: horns, guitars, and synths share harmonic space.  
- Voicings may be spread wide with synth pads, or tight unison/ octave riffs.  
- Ensemble writing often riff‑based rather than traditional horn‑section voicing.

### Key distinguishing features vs adjacent eras
- Compared to Post‑Bop: more electric instruments, straight‑eighth rock/funk grooves, heavier backbeat, more riff and vamp structures.  
- Compared to Contemporary Jazz: timbres and harmonic language rooted in 70s/80s aesthetics (analog synths, classic fusion grooves) rather than later hybrid influences and post‑modern eclecticism.

## Contemporary / Modern Jazz (1990s–present)

### Chord vocabulary
- Very broad palette:  
  - traditional chords: maj7, m7, 7, m7b5, dim7,  
  - modern colours: maj7#11, add9, m9, m11, mMaj7, 7sus(b9/#9/#11/b13), quartal and quintal stacks, clusters, polychords, hybrid voicings.  
- Heavy use of slash chords and non‑functional colours.  
- Harmonic language influenced by post‑bop, fusion, classical, and world music.

### Common progressions
- Mix of:  
  - functional ii–V–I,  
  - extended modal areas,  
  - non‑functional progressions and tonal centers,  
  - complex forms with metric and harmonic modulation.  
- Standards appear but are reharmonised; many originals have unique forms.

### Harmonic rhythm
- Highly variable: can switch between static vamps, medium‑speed functional changes, and complex, rapidly shifting harmony.  
- Sections with few chords per bar alternate with dense reharmonised passages.

### Rhythmic feel and tempo range (BPM)
- Feels include: swing, straight‑eighth, funk, hip‑hop, odd‑meter grooves, Afro‑Cuban, Brazilian, mixed meters, and hybrid feels.  
- Tempos from slow ballads to extreme uptempo; interest often lies in subdivision and metric design rather than raw speed.  
- Polyrhythms, metric modulation, and displaced accents are common.

### Bass line style
- Acoustic and electric both common, sometimes in the same project.  
- Walking, pedal, broken time, ostinatos, and groove‑based lines all appear.  
- Bass can be highly interactive, often functioning as a melodic voice rather than pure foundation.

### Drum pattern characteristics
- Very interactive, textural, and style‑blending:  
  - swing ride, broken time, backbeats, hip‑hop‑influenced grooves, world rhythms.  
  - frequent metric games and dynamic shaping.  
- Drums often co‑shape the form and energy rather than simply keep time.

### Horn voicing density
- Wide range: sparse chamber‑like settings to dense large ensembles.  
- Writing includes traditional voicings, cluster textures, counterpoint, and orchestrated lines influenced by contemporary classical and film music.  
- Use of extended techniques and timbral effects is common.

### Key distinguishing features vs adjacent eras
- Compared to Fusion: broader stylistic mix beyond rock/funk; less tied to 70s/80s electric sound; more influence from hip‑hop, world music, and modern classical.  
- Compared to earlier eras in general: maximal stylistic pluralism, high tolerance for hybrid forms, and deliberate blending of historical vocabularies in a single piece.
